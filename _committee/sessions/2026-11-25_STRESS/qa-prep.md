## Case 1

**V1 · "Mornings are 44% of SuperCoffee's defects — isn't that just when people order coffee?"**
"Fair point: 44% is the share of defects, not the error rate, and the data has no orders by hour. I use it to decide where the bag check starts, because that's where most defects are. In week one I'd ask SuperCoffee for orders by hour to confirm whether mornings are also worse in rate."

**V2 · "Would you really pause delivery for a chain that's already below its sales target?"**
"Only as the last step, decided with the AM, and only for a store that hasn't improved at all after four weeks. The ladder starts with the reward: accurate stores rank higher. The goal is to fix six stores, not punish the chain."

**V3 · "What does your week and month look like?"** (the brief asks for it explicitly)
"Every week: a 30-minute call with the six worst Chi-chi stores, the top-20 SuperCoffee ranking to their ops lead, and a Friday report to both AMs with the rate, best and worst store. Every month: a review with each POC on the rate against the path to 1.5%, refunds paid and which stores move up or down the ladder."

**V4 · "Your two plans get to 1.91%. The target is 1.5% in six months."**
"Right, and that's why the slide shows both lines. My two plans close about half the gap. Reaching 1.5% by month 6 needs all four chains from month 1. The biggest lever is The Mex Food going back to the 1.08% it already ran in September, so in week one I'd find out what changed in October. If phase 2 starts a month late we land around 1.57%."

**Rounding, if someone recomputes:** phase 2 closes "about 2,000" defects a month; all four chains land at 1.50% within rounding (7,387 vs 7,371).

## Case 2

**V5 · "Where does 100h per champion come from?"**
"The brief gives hours per feature but no capacity, so I assumed 100h, because a champion also has meetings and BAU. Even with a full-time month (~160h), 220h for DeliveryX in May doesn't fit. And the one capacity the brief does give, CommOps at 20h, is tighter: minor releases take 6h, leaving 14h for seven launches."

**V6 · "Are Brazil BYOC trips worth the same as Mexico Cash trips?"**
"Not necessarily, and I compared volume, not margin. But my reason for BYOC isn't only trips per hour: it frees April–May from 220h to 140h, and it protects Brazil, where Uber itself named courier supply from competitors as the issue. BYOC uses iFood's couriers."

**V7 · "You say order accuracy is your guardrail. How do you hold iFood's couriers to it?"**
"I can't control them, so it goes into the partnership: shared accuracy and delivery-time metrics and a joint weekly review in the first months. If quality drops, scope stays small until it recovers. That's why 'delivery quality we don't control' is on my risk row."

**V11 · (peer) "You want my Platform hours in May. What do I get back?"**
"A clear end date, and DeliveryX covers one of your minor releases later in the half. Plus visibility: it's agreed in the weekly champions review, not asked for in a hallway."

**Cash follow-up · "Isn't Cash worth more trips?"**
"Cash moves to H2, it doesn't disappear. It needs settlement, fraud controls and a courier safety protocol designed before launch anyway, and Mexico is moving toward cards, which Uber is pushing with the Klar card."

## Q6 and closing

**V8 · "What does 'compete on reliability' mean as a product decision?"**
"It's not 'no promotions', it's 'don't match every coupon'. In practice: order accuracy as a guardrail on every launch, the Case 1 plans on the chains with the most defects, and a weekly review of competitor moves with the city GMs. Uber's own Q3 call puts reliability next to selection and affordability."

**Contrarian · "Eaters choose on price."**
"Many do, and Uber competes on affordability too. My point is that a wrong order costs the eater the meal and costs the merchant the refund, and coupons don't fix that. Fast and wrong is still a bad order."
